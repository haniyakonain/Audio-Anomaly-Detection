How the model performs, explained

This page walks through what the model does and how to read each chart below, written so anyone (not just an ML engineer) can follow it.

🧠 How the detector works

Each audio clip is first passed through YAMNet, a sound-recognition network pretrained by Google on millions of everyday sounds. YAMNet turns the clip into a sequence of numeric "fingerprints" that describe what the sound contains.

That sequence is then fed into a GRU (a type of recurrent neural network that is good at learning patterns over time). It was trained on labeled machine recordings to tell healthy machine sound apart from abnormal machine sound, and it outputs a single probability from 0 to 1: how likely the clip is abnormal.

{% if 'accuracy' in stem %} 📈 Training accuracy

Is the model actually learning?

What it shows: two lines tracking how often the model's Normal/Abnormal call matched the true label, epoch by epoch. The blue line is performance on the data it trained on; the orange line is performance on data it never saw during training.

How to read it: both lines climbing and settling near the top means the model is genuinely learning the difference, not just guessing. If the orange (validation) line stayed flat while blue kept climbing, that would mean the model was memorizing instead of learning.

What happened here: training accuracy rises steadily to about 80%, and validation accuracy rises to around 72–74% before leveling off. The gap between the two lines is expected and modest, so the model generalizes reasonably well to audio it hasn't seen before.

{% elif 'loss' in stem %} 📉 Training loss

How confident and correct are the predictions?

What it shows: "loss" is a penalty score, lower is better. It punishes the model more when it is confidently wrong, and less when it is close to correct. Same two lines as the accuracy chart: training (blue) versus unseen validation data (orange).

How to read it: a steady downward trend means the model keeps improving. Bumps in the orange line are normal (real-world audio is noisy); a sustained upward trend while blue keeps dropping would be a sign of overfitting.

What happened here: training loss falls steadily to about 0.42. Validation loss trends down overall but wobbles around epochs 5 and 10–12, a mild, expected amount of noise for a dataset this size rather than a sign the model broke.

{% elif 'roc' in stem %} 🎯 ROC curve

How well can it tell normal from abnormal?

What it shows: the model doesn't just output "Normal" or "Abnormal", it outputs a probability, and a threshold decides the cutoff. This curve plots, at every possible cutoff, how many real faults get caught (vertical axis) against how many false alarms that costs (horizontal axis).

How to read it: a curve that hugs the top-left corner is ideal (catches faults with almost no false alarms). The dashed diagonal line is what pure random guessing would look like. AUC (area under the curve) compresses the whole curve into one number from 0.5 (random) to 1.0 (perfect).

What happened here: AUC = 0.769, meaning that given one random normal clip and one random abnormal clip, the model ranks the abnormal one as riskier about 77% of the time, a solidly-better-than-random detector with clear room to improve.

{% elif 'confusion' in stem %} 🧮 Confusion matrix

What actually happened on real test clips?

What it shows: a scoreboard of every prediction the model made on 837 held-out test clips at the app's current decision threshold (0.45), split by what the clip really was (rows) versus what the model guessed (columns).

How to read it: the top-left and bottom-right cells are correct calls; the top-right and bottom-left cells are mistakes. Bright/yellow means a large count.

What happened here: of 613 truly normal clips, 459 were correctly cleared and 154 triggered a false alarm. Of 224 truly abnormal clips, 142 were correctly caught and 82 slipped through undetected. In plain terms: the model currently catches about 6 in 10 real faults, and correctly clears about 3 in 4 healthy machines, useful as an early-warning aid, not yet a replacement for human inspection.
